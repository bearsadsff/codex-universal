Why do students turn to generative AI? Many people assume that students are lazy or unmotivated and are simply looking for the quickest solution to a problem. While that may be true for some, it overlooks a much larger group of students who genuinely want to learn but are trying to avoid the mental exhaustion that school can bring. Many students are balancing work and other responsibilities, which is increasingly common, and the cost of living leaves them with little choice. Rent is higher, groceries cost more, and wages have not kept up. The cost of living has increased by about 20% since 2020, and students feel that pressure. It shows up in the classroom.

Many students are forced to balance school with full-time or near full-time work and are working more than they would like. About 40% of full-time college students have jobs, and many of them work 20 or more hours a week. That level of commitment is difficult to sustain. Time is lost, and academic goals are often sacrificed. Students are faced with heavy reading assignments and dense academic writing, and they end up choosing between work and sleep. In those situations, time-saving tools can feel more necessary than concerns about academic integrity. Learning how to use AI or similar tools is often more accessible than learning how to manage anxiety or unrealistic demands. It is not much different from using CliffsNotes or looking for quick explanations on platforms like Quora or Reddit when there is no time to work through a journal article.

There is also constant uncertainty about the future. News coverage often focuses on the job market, automation, and the growing demand for technical skills. Schools have not always made expectations clear. In one setting, students are encouraged to use AI as a tool. In another, it is considered a violation of academic integrity. This inconsistency creates confusion. Given rising levels of student burnout, it is not surprising that many students feel pressure to find shortcuts just to keep up.

This does not mean concerns about AI use are without merit. When students rely too heavily on AI, it can reinforce teachers' concerns about disengagement. If students begin to feel that school is only about outcomes rather than learning, motivation can decline. Schools need clearer, more consistent guidelines about what is acceptable use of AI and what support is available for students who fall behind. Greater consistency across instructors would reduce confusion and anxiety.

Teaching media literacy is also essential. When students see that AI can produce incorrect or misleading information, they need to learn how to evaluate and use it responsibly. Simply telling students not to use AI is ineffective. Showing them how it can support brainstorming, studying, and real-world problem solving is more productive and helps them feel capable rather than restricted.

I used AI once during a time when everything felt overwhelming, and I still failed the course. I did not use it again. When I retook the course with better support and more stability in my life, I no longer felt the need to rely on shortcuts. Most students do not want to cheat. They want to learn without feeling like they are drowning. AI is not evidence of student apathy. It reflects an educational system that often sets high expectations without providing enough support.
